Writing

Most Kind Mechanics pieces start with something small and annoying.

The meeting that somehow produced no decision. The notification with no owner. The light you cannot stop noticing. The process with three extra steps because “that's just how it works”. The perfectly clean glass that still does not feel like just a glass.

Follow the irritation far enough and there is usually a system underneath it.

The writing here is about finding that system, giving the friction a name, and working out what would make it easier.

When a glass isn't just a glass

Some minds do not receive an object, rule or symbol stripped of its context. The history, associations, contradictions and meaning can arrive with it.

When Is A Glass Not Just A Glass? explores what I called causal depth perception: why some people notice the whole map around a thing, and what that can mean for the way we design tools, workplaces and communication.

Further out

The Soft Room With No Exit

Kind Mechanics sometimes follows the same question into another kind of system: where is the hidden cost landing?

The Soft Room With No Exit looks at AI assistants doing support-shaped work. The interface can be patient, gentle and immediately useful while the larger interaction quietly creates another kind of dependency or friction.

It is further from the usual workplace material, but the design question is the same: a system should not merely feel kind at the surface. The mechanics underneath it have to be kind too.

Research

Workplace and cognition research

Kind Mechanics carries the practical workplace-and-cognition strand of my research: questions about where friction comes from, how load accumulates, and what changes when the environment is treated as part of the problem.

Executive paralysis and initiation failure

Why can someone know what needs doing, want to do it, and still be unable to start?

This work looks at initiation failure as a problem to understand rather than a character judgement to apply. The practical question is what changes between “I should do this” and actually being able to move, and whether recognising that state gives us better ways to help.

Demand avoidance and institutional misfit

Workplaces often assume that motivation behaves in one particular way: set an expectation, attach an incentive or consequence, and effort should follow.

That assumption does not fit everybody. This work asks what happens when the motivational system a workplace expects is different from the one a person actually has, and how much apparent individual difficulty is really a bad fit between person and institution. Read the SocArXiv preprint →

Burnout, shutdown and cumulative load

Burnout rarely begins on the day somebody finally stops coping.

This strand looks at what accumulates beforehand: sensory cost, masking, ambiguity, interruption, recovery debt and the repeated work of adapting yourself to an environment that never quite adapts back.

The useful question is not only why somebody reached the limit. It is what kept adding to the bill.
